The Vedda word record,
and where it stops

Every Vedda word four collectors wrote down between 1863 and 1972, with who recorded it, when, and on which page, so any form can be checked against the original scan.

This is an archive, not a course. No living speaker was consulted. There is no audio of Vedda in the public domain, so nothing here can teach pronunciation beyond the two lines the collectors themselves wrote down. What follows is what the published record holds up to 1972, not the language spoken at Dambana today.

Two collectors, forty-six years apart

Thirty-nine meanings were written down by both men. Differing characters are marked, so you can see the disagreement rather than take it on trust.

A plain string comparison reports thirteen disagreements and eleven of them are false. Bailey wrote Vedda in English spelling conventions and Parker in a scholarly one: Doongah against Dun-ga, Metchan against Meccan. Publishing that as “the sources disagree” would report our own method as a fact about the language, so the two conventions are folded together before scoring.

The eight left over are marked unresolved, never “different words”. Some are visibly scan damage; others may be real variation between communities. Nothing separates them automatically, so nothing here claims to.

What three records do to each other

De Silva’s 1972 fieldwork is the modern record and the only one here gathered from living speakers. Sometimes it settles what the Victorians guessed at. Sometimes it shows they were arguing with each other and still are.

These forms are written in ASJPcode, which is not a spelling. It is a reduced phonetic alphabet: 3 is a schwa, E an open-mid front vowel, C the ch sound, ~ nasalisation. Comparing it letter by letter against Parker’s or Bailey’s romanisations would be meaningless, so the character-level marking used above is deliberately not applied here.

What the record will not support

Each of these is a constraint the sources impose, not a decision about scope.

No pronunciation

Two sentences exist in the entire public record. Parker: “the letter c is pronounced like the English ch in church.” Bailey: “vowels to be pronounced as in Italian.” Nothing else. No recording, no phonetic key, nothing to generate from.

The jungle vocabulary is not Vedda

Parker’s table sets a column of Kaele-basa beside the Vedda one: a Sinhalese hunters’ taboo register used by a different people imitating Veddas while hunting. Reading the table naively publishes Sinhalese hunting words as Vedda. It is kept as labelled comparison only.

Two thirds is not Parker’s

163 of his 248 entries carry the mark N., which he defines as taken from Hugh Nevill’s writings. One book, two collectors. Every row records which, and the set is never called “Parker’s word list”.

A blank is a meaning

Where Parker records no Vedda word, he says the Veddas simply use the Sinhalese one. Twenty such meanings are held separately rather than shown as gaps. An absence in this record is a fact about the language, not missing data.

The coverage is a floor

Parker rates his own work: “the Vaedi vocabulary which I append is extremely deficient, but that of the Kaele-basa is nearly complete.” The coloniser’s record of Sinhalese hunters imitating Vedda speech is better than his record of Vedda speech.

The classification stays open

Parker argues in 1909 that Vedda is “to a great extent the colloquial Sinhalese tongue, but slightly changed in form and accent”, and his Sinhalese column is the evidence. Whether it is a creole, a dialect or an isolate is published as a live debate, not settled here.

Twelve settlements, and who said what where

Seligmann tags most forms with the village it was recorded in. Neither Parker nor Bailey does that, which makes this the only source that shows the language varying across places rather than across decades.

Dambani is Dambana, the settlement the Vedda community still lives in and the one every account of them now starts from. It supplies more recorded forms than anywhere else.

Every Seligmann entry on this page links to the page image on archive.org, so any form can be checked against the original rather than taken on our word.

The book, the copyright, and what was actually taken

Seligmann’s The Veddas (1911) holds the richest vocabulary of the four. It is public domain in the United States and still protected in the UK and Sri Lanka, so what was taken from it was chosen to be defensible in all three.

Joint authorship is confirmed from the title page itself, not from a catalogue record:

THE VEDDAS BY C. G. SELIGMANN, M.D. LECTURER IN ETHNOLOGY IN THE UNIVERSITY OF LONDON AND BRENDA Z. SELIGMANN WITH A CHAPTER BY C. S. MYERS, M.D., D.Sc. AND AN APPENDIX BY A. MENDIS GUNASEKARA, Mudaliar Cambridge: at the University Press, 1911

That matters, because if the book were C. G. Seligmann’s alone it would have entered the public domain in 2011 and this whole exclusion would be wrong. It is not: the term runs from the last surviving author. Brenda Zara Seligman died on 2 January 1965, confirmed by the Pitt Rivers Museum, which holds the Seligman collection, and by Wikipedia. One archive catalogue records 1960; two independent sources including the holding institution outweigh it, and either way the date has not yet passed.

What is actually locked up

Around 185 numbered entries over 28 pages, and richer than anything we have: each entry carries several forms tagged by locality (which Vedda settlement each was recorded in), which neither Parker nor Bailey records at all. Gunasekara then derives them against Sinhalese, Sanskrit, Tamil, Hindi and Marathi.

No earlier public-domain version exists. The preface says only some plates had appeared previously, in Man; the vocabulary had not. And the Seligmanns state the word list is their own: “the words are given as we took them down in the field.” Gunasekara’s derivations and Myers’s music chapter are separately credited contributions, but neither is the vocabulary.

What was taken, and what was left behind

A word and its meaning is a fact, and facts are not copyrightable anywhere. What copyright protects is the compilation, its arrangement, and the expressive prose. No UK database right applies either: that covers databases made after 1983 and runs fifteen years, so an 1911 compilation has none. The extraction is drawn along exactly that line:

  • Taken: the attested forms, their English gloss, the locality codes, and the cognate each entry cites. Facts.
  • Not taken: Gunasekara’s etymological derivations and every other analytical passage. The parser cuts each entry at the first derivation marker and never reads past it. That is the part copyright actually protects.
  • Not copied: Seligmann’s arrangement. Rows are merged into our own concept scheme beside the other three sources, never republished in his order.
  • Linked instead: every row points at the page image on archive.org, which already hosts the scan lawfully. Readers are sent to the original rather than served a copy.

US public-domain status does not travel with the app. Copyright is territorial, so publishing as a US company from US hosting clears the extraction and US distribution, and does not by itself clear making the work available in the UK or Sri Lanka. That is why the line above is drawn at facts rather than at jurisdiction.

A trap for whoever checks this next: the 1911 title page spells it SELIGMANN, with two n’s. In later life both used Seligman. Catalogue searches disagree because of it, and it is why one record lists only C. G. as author.

Sources

Parker, H. (1909)

Ancient Ceylon: an account of the aborigines and of part of the early civilization. London: Luzac & Co., pp. 123-132.

Four columns: English, Sinhalese, Kaele-basa, Vaedi. Author died 1926, so public domain everywhere. 242 forms, 129 meanings.

Bailey, J. (1863)

An Account of the Wild Tribes of the Veddahs of Ceylon. Transactions of the Ethnological Society of London, vol. II, pp. 278-320.

Four columns: English, Veddah, Singhalese, Remarks, and the remarks are Bailey’s own etymology on 61 entries. Author died 1888, so public domain outright.

Seligmann & Seligmann (1911)

The Veddas. Cambridge University Press, Vedda Vocabulary pp. 423-451.

The only source that records which settlement each form came from, across twelve villages. Public domain in the United States; protected in the UK and Sri Lanka until 1 January 2036. Facts extracted, derivations left in the book, page images linked.

De Silva, M. W. S. (1972)

Vedda language of Ceylon: texts and lexicon. München: R. Kitzinger. Münchener Studien zur Sprachwissenschaft 7.

The book is in copyright and none of it is reproduced here. These 42 forms come from the ASJP Database v21 (Wichmann, Holman, Brown, Dryer & Ran, eds., 2025), which publishes them under CC BY 4.0 and names De Silva 1972 as their source. Wordlist compiled by Viveka Velupillai.
